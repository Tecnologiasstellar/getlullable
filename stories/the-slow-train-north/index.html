The Slow Train North

▶ 38 minSlow FictionWanderingread by Nora VancePremium

The 21:14 to the north takes eleven hours because it has never once considered taking fewer. A traveller — we are never told their business, and it never matters — settles into berth 4C with a thermos of tea and the particular contentment of a person whose only remaining obligation is to be carried. Stations arrive at long intervals, announce themselves softly, and are gone.

The ending, given away now: the train arrives. On time, at dawn, without incident. The traveller sleeps through the arrival, which the conductor considers the sign of a journey done properly.

The first minute

The train left at fourteen minutes past nine with the small ceremonial shudder that sleeper trains permit themselves, once, at the start of a long night — a shrug of couplings settling into the work ahead. The traveller in berth 4C poured the first cup of tea while the city lights were still practising their reflections in the window, and by the time the cup needed refilling the lights had thinned to farms, and the farms to the occasional lamp of a house whose owner was, sensibly, asleep. The window went about its business of being dark. The train went about its business of being slow…

Why this one works at night

Motion without effort, progress without plot. Eleven hours of country going by at a held-breath pace gives the mind a window to look out of instead of a problem to hold. It's the shortest story in the library, and almost nobody has heard the arrival.

“…somewhere after the third station the fields gave up their fences, and the dark got wider in a way that was easier to feel than to see, the way a room gets bigger when someone stops—”
