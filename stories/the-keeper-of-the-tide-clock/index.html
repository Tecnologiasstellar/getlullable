The Keeper of the Tide Clock

▶ 52 minFolkloreDreamingread by Marguerite BellPremium

Above the harbourmaster's door in a town the story never quite names, there is a clock with one hand and no numbers, and it is never wrong — provided you understand what it is measuring, which almost nobody does. Its keeper, a patient woman with the unhurried authority of someone who has explained the same beautiful thing for thirty years, is happy to walk you through it. The moon, she'll tell you, keeps its own hours. The clock simply agrees with the moon.

The ending, given away now: the tide comes in, as promised. Then it goes out, also as promised. The clock is right both times, and the keeper locks up and walks home along the seawall.

The first minute

The clock above the harbourmaster's door had one hand, no numbers, and the settled expression of an instrument that has stopped arguing with anyone. Visitors assumed it was broken. The keeper had long ago stopped correcting them on the spot, preferring to wait for the ones who came back to ask — because a person who comes back to ask about a clock is a person with an evening to spare, and evenings to spare were the only currency she had ever charged. The tide, she would begin, is a clock older than clocks. It keeps lunar time, which runs at its own dignified lag…

Why this one works at night

An explanation with no exam at the end — the mechanics of lunar time delivered like a bedtime confidence. It scratches the curious brain's itch to learn while promising, in the same warm breath, that none of it will be needed in the morning.

“…a tide clock gains and loses against the sun on purpose, she said, because the moon runs its own office and keeps its own hours, roughly twelve and a half to the—”
