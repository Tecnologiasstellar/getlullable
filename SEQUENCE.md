# Onboarding sequence — letters 2–5

Issue №001 lives in [NEWSLETTER-01.md](NEWSLETTER-01.md). These four run on the permission ladder in [MARKETING.md](MARKETING.md) §3 — each asks for slightly more and returns slightly more. **Never skip a rung to hit a number.**

Voice throughout: academic, deeply warm, unhurried — real scholarship at low volume, the register of a knowledgeable friend talking quietly because someone in the next room is asleep. Dark-mode delivery per the spec in NEWSLETTER-01. One link maximum, always at the bottom. Sent Sundays 9pm local, one week apart, so a new subscriber's onboarding *is* the newsletter — no parallel drip running alongside the real thing. Two letters in one week breaks the first promise on the signup form, and the promise is worth more than the tempo.

## Letter 2 — the worldview

**Subject:** `Why you failed at meditating`
**Preview:** `You didn't.`
**Send:** +7 days
**Job:** Convert a subscriber into a believer. This is the most-forwarded email; write it to be forwarded to one specific person, not to a list.

---

Good evening.

A short digression this week, and then we'll go back to never discussing ourselves.

Some time in the last decade you probably downloaded a meditation app. Most people did. You used it eight or nine times. A calm voice asked you to notice your breath, and you noticed your breath for four seconds and then thought about an email. The voice said that was fine and to gently return. You gently returned. Then you thought about the email again.

Eventually you stopped opening the app, and somewhere in the back of your mind you filed it as a small personal failure. A thing other people can do.

Here's the thing about that: you were doing it correctly. That's the exercise. The wandering and returning *is* the practice, and it works well for a great many people.

It just doesn't work at midnight for a mind like yours.

Because a mind like yours doesn't wander idly. It wanders *productively*. It goes and finds something genuinely interesting and comes back holding it. Asking it to let go of the interesting thing is asking it to do the one thing it's least equipped for — and at 11:50pm, with nothing else in the room, it will win that argument every time.

The instruction was never wrong. It was just written for a different kind of head.

The alternative isn't emptying. It's occupying. Give that mind something true, uneventful, and going nowhere, and it will follow it out of politeness, get bored somewhere around the fourth clause, and put itself down.

That's the entire theory. It is not very sophisticated. It's just pointed at the right people.

Next week: a fact, as usual, and nothing about us.

Sleep well,
Lullable

## Letter 3 — the preference (rung 2)

**Subject:** `Four kinds of quiet`
**Preview:** `Pick one. Or don't; we'll keep sending everything.`
**Send:** +14 days
**Job:** The smallest possible ask. A click, no typing, no account. It doubles as our first real segmentation and — more usefully — it makes the reader commit to a taste, which makes the next ask easier.

---

Good evening.

This week, a small piece of housekeeping, and then a fact.

Calm, it turns out, has genres. We've found four, and readers are unexpectedly firm about which one carries them out:

**Engineered** — canals, gauges, pallets, the long peace of Roman concrete.
**Natural** — sediment, lichen, tidal records, the migration habits of unremarkable birds.
**Historical** — minutes, timetables, the 1846 Act, committees that met for nine years.
**Cosmic** — orbital mechanics, cataloguing, very large numbers described very slowly.

[Pick yours.] One click. You can change it whenever, and if you ignore this entirely we'll keep sending everything, which is what most people do.

Now, this week's fact.

**The shipping pallet was standardised in 1968, and the argument took eleven years.**

The dispute was not about strength or material. It was about size. American pallets were forty-eight inches by forty. European pallets were eighty centimetres by a hundred and twenty. Neither measurement was arrived at by testing anything; both were arrived at by already existing.

The International Organization for Standardization resolved this in the usual manner, which was to approve six sizes and declare the matter settled. It is still six sizes. Roughly half a billion pallets are made every year, in six sizes, because in 1968 nobody could be made to give up their size.

—

Sleep well,

## Letter 4 — the trial (rung 3)

**Subject:** `Seven nights`
**Preview:** `No card. It stops on its own.`
**Send:** +21 days
**Job:** The first commercial ask, three weeks in. Soft, one link, bottom of the email. The fact comes first — the offer is a footnote to it, deliberately. If this email reads like a sales email, it has failed; a subscriber who forwards issue 2 and then gets a hard pitch feels traded.

---

Good evening.

The fact first, as usual.

**Between 1883 and 1904, two men lived at the summit of Ben Nevis and took a weather reading every hour.**

Every hour. Through twenty-one winters, at four thousand four hundred feet, in cloud roughly three hundred days a year. Temperature, pressure, wind, humidity, and a note on the state of the sky. If the instruments iced over, they went outside and cleared them and took the reading anyway.

The observatory closed in 1904 for lack of funding. The readings — one and a half million of them — were published in four volumes that almost nobody opened for a century.

They are now among the most valuable long-run climate records in Britain, for the simple reason that somebody wrote down something boring, on time, for twenty-one years.

—

That last part is the closest thing to a moral we've ever printed. We regret it slightly.

If you'd like this sort of thing read to you at length: the app opens for seven nights, no card, and stops by itself at the end. There is nothing to cancel. [Seven nights.]

Sleep well,
Lullable

## Letter 5 — the mechanic

**Subject:** `The last thing you heard`
**Preview:** `You will not remember it. That's the receipt.`
**Send:** +28 days
**Job:** Introduce the share mechanic — to trialists *and* to people who never started one, because the card is funny whether or not you use the product, and a subscriber who never subscribes is still a person who forwards things.

---

Good evening.

A note on the strangest part of what we do.

The app can tell roughly when you've gone. The phone stops moving, the room stops changing, and the audio keeps going into an empty room for another half hour. So in the morning it shows you one thing: the exact sentence you were listening to when you stopped listening.

It's almost always cut mid-clause. Something like:

> *"…the standard gauge of four feet eight and a half inches was chosen because it was the width of the wagons already in use at the Killingworth colliery, which had themselves been built to match the—"*

And underneath: *38 minutes on railway gauges.*

We didn't set out to build a funny thing. We built it because people kept asking whether the app had worked, and the honest answer turned out to be a timestamp and a severed sentence.

It has become the only part anyone shares. Which we've made peace with, because a screenshot of the moment your brain gave up on the width of a colliery wagon is a more persuasive advertisement than anything we could write, and it has the advantage of being true.

Next week: back to normal. Something about lichen.

Sleep well,
Lullable
