Good evening.

This is the first letter, so, briefly, the arrangement: every Sunday night we send you three short paragraphs about something genuinely fascinating, written to be read in bed, and then we stop. There is no news in these letters, nothing to act on, and no quiz at any point. You are not required to remember any of it. That's rather the point.

This week: the thermal patience of Roman concrete.

The Pantheon's dome has stood in the middle of Rome for nineteen centuries, unreinforced — no steel, no rebar, nothing in tension — and it remains the largest dome of its kind ever built. The Romans made their concrete from lime and a volcanic ash called *pozzolana*, and they mixed it, we now believe, hot: quicklime and water reacting in the mortar itself, leaving tiny white fragments of lime scattered through the material like sea salt through bread. For centuries, engineers assumed those fragments were sloppy mixing. They were the opposite. When a crack forms and rainwater finds its way in, it dissolves a little of the lime, which recrystallizes and quietly seals the crack behind it. The building repairs itself, slowly, in the rain, and has been doing so since the emperor Hadrian.

And the material is patient in a second way. Masonry that thick has enormous thermal inertia — it absorbs the day's heat so gradually, and releases it so slowly, that the inside of a Roman bath stayed temperate while the afternoon outside blazed and the night outside froze. The walls were, in effect, several hours behind the weather, and entirely unbothered by it. Some of the harbour piers the Romans poured directly into the sea are stronger today than the day they set, because seawater has spent two thousand years growing new minerals through them. Most things are worn down by time and weather. This material has an arrangement with both.

That's all for tonight. There will be no follow-up questions.

Real scholarship at low volume — the register of a knowledgeable friend talking quietly because someone in the next room is asleep. Never write "but here's the fascinating part": if it needs signposting, it isn't. Endings and conclusions given away early; no suspense, ever.

**Structure to repeat:**

1. A one-line greeting and the week's subject, plainly stated.
2. **Exactly three paragraphs** of the fact-world. First paragraph may carry the marvel; the last should land on the calmest available note.
3. A one-line close that releases the reader — *"There will be no follow-up questions."*
