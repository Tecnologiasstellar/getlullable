The cognitive shuffle: the sleep trick for overthinkers

August 14, 2026 · 3 min read

The short answer

The cognitive shuffle is a bedtime technique in which you deliberately imagine a series of random, unrelated, emotionally neutral things — a bucket, a bridge, a banana — holding each picture in mind for a few seconds before moving to the next. It was developed by Luc Beaudoin, a cognitive scientist at Simon Fraser University, and the idea is deceptively simple: coherent thinking keeps you awake, so you give your mind something to do that deliberately refuses to cohere. It is free, requires nothing, and you can try it tonight.

Why incoherence is the point

Most bedtime advice tries to quiet the mind. The shuffle does something stranger and, for overthinkers, far more achievable: it scrambles it.

Beaudoin's reasoning starts from what a mind naturally does in the last minutes before sleep. Left to itself, thinking at sleep onset goes loose and pictorial — disconnected images, half-scenes, a drifting sequence that follows no argument and reaches no conclusion. Nobody plans their Tuesday in that state. It arrives on its own, and it arrives just before sleep does.

His insight was that this loose, imagistic drifting isn't only a symptom of falling asleep. It may also be a signal. A brain running incoherent, goal-free imagery is a brain with no urgent problem open — and that is precisely the condition under which it is safe to let go. Coherent, verbal, goal-directed thinking says the opposite: we are still working on something.

So the shuffle counterfeits the good state. You manufacture the drifting on purpose, and the sleep system, reading the room, gets on with its job.

How to do it tonight

The method takes about fifteen seconds to learn:

  • Pick a neutral word. Something concrete and unloaded — candle, harbour, bedtime. Nothing that belongs to your job or your family.
  • Take its first letter, and think of words starting with that letter. Candle → cactus, canoe, curtain, cobblestone.
  • Picture each one for a few seconds — actually see it, don't just recite it. A canoe, pulled up on gravel. A curtain, moving slightly.
  • When that letter runs dry or gets boring, move to the next letter of your original word, and carry on.
  • Never chase meaning. If two images connect into a story, break it. If a word opens a worry — a bill, a name, an email — drop it and take another. Disconnection is the entire mechanism.

You are not trying to succeed at anything. There is no correct number of images, no point at which you've done it properly. The drifting is the technique, and losing your place is how it ends.

What the research actually shows

The evidence is real but modest, and worth describing accurately. In a randomized study of 154 students who complained of a racing mind at bedtime, Beaudoin's serial diverse imagining task was compared against structured problem-solving — the well-established practice of writing your worries down before bed. The shuffle performed about as well, with participants reporting reduced pre-sleep arousal, less "sleep effort," and better sleep quality.

Read that with the appropriate calm. It's a student sample, mostly self-reported, measuring an outcome that is famously hard to measure. It sits alongside a much larger literature showing that what keeps people awake is the kind of thinking they're doing — planning, rehearsing, relitigating — rather than any failure of relaxation. What the shuffle offers is a mechanism for changing the kind, which is a more tractable problem than changing the amount.

It is not a treatment, and persistent insomnia deserves a clinician rather than a technique. But as a thing to try tonight, at no cost, it is among the most sensible ideas in the whole field.
