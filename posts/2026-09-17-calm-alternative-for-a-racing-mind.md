A Calm alternative is worth looking for when the thing keeping you awake is your own
commentary rather than a general shortage of relaxation. Calm is a wellness platform —
meditation courses, breathwork, music, daily check-ins, streaks — with a sleep-story shelf
inside it. If the sleep stories are the only part you ever open, the subscription is
$14.99 a month or $69.99 a year for a library you mostly do not use, in an app that is
also built to bring you back tomorrow. The alternatives worth considering are narrower,
cheaper, and sometimes free.

## What Calm is good at, said properly

This is the part most comparison pages skip, so: Calm is very good, and if you use both
halves of it there is no argument here worth listening to. Its own App Store listing
describes more than a hundred exclusive Sleep Stories with new ones added monthly,
narrated by people you have heard of — Matthew McConaughey, Stephen Fry, Bob Ross. The
production is expensive and sounds it. There is no advertising anywhere in it, and some
of the app stays free forever. For a reader who also wants a guided meditation on a bad
Tuesday afternoon, one subscription covering both is the sensible purchase, and switching
away from it to save a few dollars would be a false economy.

## Where the mismatch shows up

The mismatch is not quality. It is that sleep is one shelf in a shop selling several
things, and that shop has to work as a shop.

Calm's listing names daily streaks and a minutes tracker among its features, which is
ordinary for the category and entirely rational — an app that is opened daily is an app
that renews. But a streak is a small obligation, and checking at 23:40 whether you have
kept one is the opposite of what the last ten minutes of the day are for. The mechanism
is the same one that makes [podcasts keep you awake when you are
exhausted](/sleep/why-do-podcasts-keep-me-awake/): anything that asks something of you,
however gently, is asking you to stay.

The second consequence is simpler. If you open one shelf, you are paying shop prices.

## Is the Calm app worth it?

Honestly, it depends on which half you use, and it is not a close call either way.

For context on what the category costs: Headspace's listing gives $12.99 a month and
$69.99 a year, and says plainly that those are United States prices. Calm and Headspace
are within a couple of dollars of each other because they are selling the same shape of
thing — a platform, priced as a platform. Neither is overcharging for what it contains.
The question is only whether what it contains is what you came for. If you meditate in
the morning and listen at night, yes, obviously. If the app is a bedtime audio player
that happens to have a meditation course attached, you are paying platform rates for a
narrow use, and the honest answer is that something smaller will serve you better.

## Sleep stories like Calm's, from elsewhere

What to actually look for, if you go narrower. Offline download, because a stream that
stalls at 2am is its own interruption. No advertising, for the structural reason rather
than the irritating one. No streak, no daily check-in, nothing that turns going to bed
into a thing you can fail at. A sleep timer that fades rather than stops. And a narrator
who is deliberately unremarkable — [the qualities that make a voice work at
night](/sleep/boring-voices-to-fall-asleep-to/) are close to the opposite of the
qualities that sell an app.

The free options are genuinely fine and deserve saying out loud. A long, dull podcast
downloaded before bed. Public-domain audiobooks. The shipping forecast, which has been
[doing this job for people since long before anyone monetised
it](/sleep/the-shipping-forecast-at-0048/). None of this is proprietary technology, and
[the list of things worth playing](/sleep/what-to-listen-to-when-you-cant-sleep/) is
longer than the app stores suggest.

## Where we come in, with the concessions first

Lullable is much smaller than Calm. There is no meditation, no breathwork, no course, no
celebrity, no streak, and no daily anything. The catalogue is a fraction of the size, and
if you want variety above all else, that is a real reason to buy the bigger app instead.

What it is, is one thing done narrowly: long, flat, downloadable narration for people
whose problem at night is thinking, written so that [the ending is given away before you
get there](/sleep/sleep-stories-for-adults-who-overthink/). There is a free tier and a
subscription that opens the rest, and no advertising in either, because the whole product
is a voice getting quieter and an ad break is the one thing it cannot contain.

That is the trade. Fewer things, cheaper, and nothing in the app that wants you awake.
