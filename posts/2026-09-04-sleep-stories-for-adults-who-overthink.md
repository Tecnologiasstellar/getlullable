Sleep stories for adults who overthink are audio narratives built with nothing in them to be good at. An overthinking mind does not merely fail to settle — it takes whatever it is handed and turns it into a task to be performed and assessed, including the going-to-sleep itself. So the version that suits an overthinker withholds every handle: no plot worth predicting, no ending worth waiting for, no technique to execute correctly, and no measure of how well it is going. Occupying enough to hold attention, and offering nothing to score.

## The thing overthinkers do that other people don't

Most bedtime advice is written for a mind that is merely busy. Turn the lights down, put the phone away, hand it something calm. That works, and for a lot of people it is the whole answer.

An overthinker adds a second layer on top. Whatever the material is, some part of the mind stands slightly outside it, watching: *is this working, am I doing it right, how long has it been, am I closer than I was ten minutes ago.* Handed a breathing exercise, they audit the breathing. Handed a meditation, they grade themselves on the meditation. Handed a story, they start predicting the story. The problem was never a shortage of calm input. It is that the monitor keeps running on top of it.

## Sleep is not a thing you can try at

There is a strand of research that treats this directly, and it is worth reporting at its actual size. In 2006 Espie and colleagues proposed what they called the attention–intention–effort pathway: the argument that normal sleep is a relatively automatic process, and that precisely because it is automatic, it is vulnerable to being inhibited by focused attention and by direct attempts to control it. That paper is a theoretical review — a model with evidence assembled behind it and a research agenda attached, not a finding.

The related instrument is more concrete. A year earlier, Broomfield and Espie published the first psychometric data for the Glasgow Sleep Effort Scale, built on the frequent clinical observation that patients with insomnia strive to control their sleep, and on the plain physiological fact that sleep cannot be placed under full voluntary control. Their sample was small, and they described it that way themselves. The reason to mention any of this is not that it settles the question. It is that "trying" is a describable thing you can be doing more or less of at 1am, and an overthinker is usually doing more.

That is the trap under the whole category. Effort may be the one input that makes the outcome less likely, and an overthinker's native response to a stubborn problem is more effort.

## The three handles an overthinker will grab

Given a story, a self-monitoring mind reaches for whatever it can hold. The engineering is mostly a matter of removing those.

- **A plot.** Any withheld ending converts a listener into a reader. If something is coming, you will stay for it, and part of you will be quietly working out what it is. This is [the same reason podcasts backfire](/sleep/why-do-podcasts-keep-me-awake/), only gentler and therefore harder to notice.
- **A technique.** Anything with steps has a right way of being done, and a right way invites the question of whether you are doing it. The most reliable material asks nothing of you at all — you are not participating, you are overhearing.
- **A yardstick.** Anything that lets you check your progress toward sleep gives the monitor a job. No countdown, no arc to locate yourself in, no sense of a first act ending — nothing that answers *how far along am I*.

What is left after all that is a voice describing something true, concrete and entirely non-urgent, moving at a pace with no reason to accelerate. Bedtime stories for adults who overthink are not softer than ordinary ones. They are flatter on purpose.

## What to look for

Choosing among sleep stories for overthinkers, look for an ending given away in advance, or no ending at all; a narrator who sounds unhurried rather than expressive; real subject matter rather than invented scenes, since [facts arrive already finished and fiction has to be authored as it goes](/sleep/what-to-think-about-to-fall-asleep/); and no next episode waiting. Avoid anything advertising a twist, a journey, or a destination. The description is a fair sample of the thing: if it is written to make you curious, the audio was too.

## Where we come in, honestly

None of this needs an app. You can hand your own mind a dull true subject tonight and walk through it slowly, which is what [the cognitive shuffle](/sleep/cognitive-shuffle-method/) does from the other direction.

The one cost of doing it unaided is that generating the material is mild work, and mild work is exactly what a monitoring mind will find something to evaluate in. So we read it out instead — the weather at the bottom of the sea, a Roman bathhouse at closing time, the standardisation of the railway gauge — in a flat, warm voice that gets slightly quieter every minute. There is nothing to follow correctly and no quiz in the morning. [The library shows every ending up front](/stories/), on principle, because an ending you already know is one less thing to stay awake for.
