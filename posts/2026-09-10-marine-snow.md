Marine snow is the steady downward drift of organic debris from the sunlit surface of the ocean toward the seafloor. It is made of dead plankton, faecal matter, mucus, sand, soot and other inorganic dust, clumped into pale aggregates that grow as they fall — some reaching several centimetres across. NOAA describes flakes that fall for weeks before they finally arrive. It happens everywhere in the open ocean, all the time, and it has been happening for as long as there have been things in the water to die.

## What is falling

Very little of it was ever an animal you could name. The bulk is the residue of the surface: phytoplankton that finished, the shells and fragments of protists, and a great deal of waste — among the particles photographed at depth are the faecal pellets of krill, salps and larvaceans, alongside diatom fragments and looser aggregates of detritus.

The rest is glue. Zooplankton secrete mucus in quantity, mostly salps, appendicularians and pteropods, and phytoplankton and bacteria exude natural polymers as waste. That sticky fraction is what turns a cloud of unrelated particles into a flake. A snowflake in air is one crystal growing outward. A flake of marine snow is an accident of adhesion — things that happened to touch, in water, and stayed touching.

## The fall

Nothing about the descent is direct. Most of the organic matter never gets past the first thousand metres; it is filtered, eaten and re-excreted by microbes, zooplankton and animals that live on exactly this, which is why the deep ocean has a food supply at all in a place with no light to make one.

The bookkeeping is stark. Somewhere between 1% and 40% of the ocean's primary production is exported out of the sunlit zone at the top. Only about 1% of what was made at the surface reaches the sea floor. The other ninety-nine parts are intercepted on the way down by something hungry.

There is also the pressure. A study published in 2026 put diatom aggregates into rotating tanks and raised the pressure to what a particle meets at two to six kilometres of depth; the aggregates leaked dissolved organic matter equivalent to roughly half their starting carbon. That was a tank, not an ocean. What it suggests, carefully, is that a flake may arrive at the bottom having quietly given away much of what it set out with.

## What arrives

What is left lands, and keeps landing, and becomes ooze — the formal term for a pelagic sediment that is at least 30% the microscopic remains of calcareous or siliceous plankton. It accumulates at rates that are hard to hold in the head. Calcareous ooze deepens by 0.3 to 5 centimetres per thousand years. Siliceous ooze, 0.2 to 1. The red and brown clay of the most remote abyssal plains, 0.1 to 0.5 centimetres in a millennium. Run that last figure across the whole span of recorded history and you get somewhere between half a centimetre and a couple of centimetres of mud.

Stand a metre stick upright in the slowest of it and you are looking at something close to a million years of weather, in the order it fell, undisturbed. There is [more of this at the bottom of the Mariana Trench](/sleep/the-weather-at-the-bottom-of-the-sea/), where the same snow has been settling into the deepest floor on the planet the entire time.

## Why it holds at night

People look for calming ocean facts to fall asleep to for the same reason they search for [a boring history podcast](/sleep/boring-history-podcast-to-fall-asleep-to/): they want something true, unhurried, and not about them. Marine snow qualifies almost too well. It has no plot. Nothing in it is approaching, resolving, or about to go wrong. The interesting parts — the mucus, the pressure, the arithmetic of the 1% — are interesting in the flat way a tide table is interesting, and they give themselves away in a sentence rather than being withheld for later.

That is most of what separates it from the material that [keeps a mind running](/sleep/why-cant-i-stop-thinking-at-night/) after midnight. Attention needs somewhere to be. A fact with no stake in it is somewhere to be that asks nothing back.

Right now, over every deep basin on Earth, it is snowing. It will still be snowing in the morning, and the floor will be no measurably deeper than it was tonight.
