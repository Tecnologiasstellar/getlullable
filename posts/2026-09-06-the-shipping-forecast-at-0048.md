The Shipping Forecast is a weather bulletin for the seas around the British Isles, issued by the Met Office and read out on BBC Radio 4. The late edition begins at 00:48 and finishes around 00:58. It names thirty-one sea areas in the same clockwise order every night, in a vocabulary that barely changes, and it is written to a strict limit: 350 words, raised to 380 for the late broadcast because that one includes an extra area. It is not addressed to you. That is most of what makes it what it is.

## The order

The areas go round the British Isles clockwise, and they always go in the same direction, starting in the north and working the whole way round. Viking. North Utsire. South Utsire. Forties, Cromarty, Forth, Tyne, Dogger, Fisher, German Bight. Down the east coast, along the Channel, out to Biscay, up past Ireland, and home along the top: Bailey, Fair Isle, Faeroes, Southeast Iceland.

The list is old but not fixed. North Utsire and South Utsire were carved out in August 1984 to match boundaries the neighbouring North Sea countries had agreed. In 2002, the area called Finisterre was renamed FitzRoy, for the Robert FitzRoy who founded the Met Office and began its storm-warning service for shipping in 1861. The reason given for the change was duller: the Spanish met service was already using Finisterre for somewhere else, and two Finisterres is one too many.

## The vocabulary

Wind moves clockwise or anticlockwise, and the forecast has one word for each: veering and backing. Timing has three words, each with a defined edge — imminent means within six hours, soon means six to twelve, later means twelve to twenty-four. Visibility has four settings: good, moderate, poor, fog. A gale warning is issued at force 8 and above.

Nothing is elaborated. On the morning this was written, the Met Office bulletin for Viking read: "Southerly veering southwesterly later, 4 to 6, occasionally 7 in north." Then the next area, in the same shape, with different numbers. There is no reason given, no consequence drawn, and nobody is asked how they feel about it.

## What comes first

At about 00:45, before the late forecast, Radio 4 plays "Sailing By" — a slow, circling waltz written by Ronald Binge in 1963. It exists for a practical reason: it is a station identifier for anyone tuning a radio at sea, and it is elastic, so it can be played whole or in part depending on how the schedule has run, and the forecast can still start at 00:48 exactly. It was written to fill a gap, and it is the part many listeners remember best.

## Why it holds up at night

The forecast has been going, in one form or another, for a very long time — over the Air Ministry's transmitter in Morse from January 1924, then as spoken word on the BBC from 4 July 1925, which the BBC marked as its centenary in 2025. In all that time the shape has barely moved.

What that produces is a piece of radio with no hook in it. Nothing is coming. There is no argument being built, no story with a turn in it, no host wondering what *you* think. The information is real and consequential — someone on a trawler off Rockall needs it — but the need is not yours, and being addressed by something that does not want anything from you is a rare condition on modern audio. It is the same reason [a podcast can keep you awake](/sleep/why-do-podcasts-keep-me-awake/) while [something duller holds](/sleep/boring-history-podcast-to-fall-asleep-to/): attention that isn't being pulled has somewhere quieter to settle.

It is also, straightforwardly, weather at a distance — sea state and visibility in places you are not, described flatly by someone reading a list. There is [more of that at the bottom of the ocean](/sleep/the-weather-at-the-bottom-of-the-sea/), where nothing has happened quickly for a very long time.

Tonight at 00:48, Viking will be read first and Southeast Iceland last, and the numbers between them will be different from last night's, and that will be the whole of it.
