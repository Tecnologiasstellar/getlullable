Stagira, on a green peninsula in the north of Greece, three hundred and eighty-four years before our era. A physician's son grows up around dried herbs and careful hands and the quiet habit of looking closely before deciding anything. At seventeen he walks south to Athens, to a grove of olive trees where people argue gently about enormous things, and stays for twenty years.

The ending, given away now: he dies in his fifties, on an island, of an ordinary illness, having written down more of the world than almost anyone before him. Nothing in the story is in doubt, and nothing in it needs deciding tonight.

## The first minute

> The story begins in a small town by the sea, in the north of Greece. The town was called Stagira. It sat on a green peninsula, with water on both sides, and fishing boats drawn up on the sand. The year was three hundred and eighty-four before our era. Twenty-three centuries ago, and a little more. A boy was born there, and his name was Aristotle. His father was a physician. Not a grand one, but a good one, good enough to serve a king's household in Macedonia. So the boy grew up around medicine. Around dried herbs, and careful hands, and the quiet habit of looking closely before deciding anything…

## Why this one works at night

It is a life told in order, and a life told in order has no cliffhangers — you always know roughly where you are and roughly where it goes. The questions the story raises are the oldest and least urgent ones there are: what is a good life, what is a friend, what is this. None of them can be settled before morning, and the narration never pretends otherwise. This is the free story, and the four minutes on the homepage are its opening.
