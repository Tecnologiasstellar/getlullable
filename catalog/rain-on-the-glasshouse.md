The rain began before the story did, and it does not stop. Elin, the night gardener of a Victorian glasshouse, has decided the weather has made her decision for her: she will wait it out among the ferns, with a lamp turned low and a kettle on the little stove, and she will listen. There are one hundred and four panes of glass overhead. The rain plays each one slightly differently, and Elin — unhurried, professionally patient — takes her time telling you how.

The ending, given away now: the rain eases around dawn. Elin has done nothing except drink two cups of tea and repot a fern that could have waited. This is not a criticism.

## The first minute

> The rain had settled in the way rain does when it has read the forecast and knows it is expected — steadily, without ambition, a workmanlike rain with the whole night booked. Elin heard it first on the south slope of the roof, where the panes are oldest and have gone slightly loose in their glazing bars, so that each drop lands with a small woody note, like a finger tapped on a table by someone thinking about something else. She put the kettle on the stove, because the correct response to weather of this kind is a kettle, and she sat down among the ferns to listen to the roof describe the rain to her, pane by pane…

## Why this one works at night

Real rain recordings loop, and looping is what pattern-hungry brains catch. This rain is narrated instead — an inventory of one hundred and four panes, each with its own small sound, delivered at the pace of a kettle coming slowly to the boil. There is nothing to wait for. The rain has the whole night booked, and so do you.
